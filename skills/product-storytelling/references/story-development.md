# Develop the story beyond the page

Use this reference for campaign concepts, launch scripts, films, demonstrations, and the relationship between them. Establish the product facts and governing promise with [Narrative strategy](narrative-strategy.md) first.

## Assign the communication job

Keep a lasting belief, a product claim, and a creative treatment distinct. Decide which level the assignment needs:

| Level | Working question | What keeps it credible |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Brand belief | What does this organization stand for? | Observable choices and conduct consistent with the belief |
| Campaign premise | What recurring idea makes that belief or benefit tangible? | A relevant connection to the offering, with room for different executions |
| Launch proposition | What is new, for whom, and why does it matter? | A precise difference the available product can substantiate |
| Demonstration | What can someone see the product actually do? | Representative behavior with conditions and limits intact |
| Decision material | What must someone know to judge fit? | Accessible details, tradeoffs, requirements, and a clear next step |

Use only the levels needed. A small product does not require a manifesto. An atmospheric film may help people identify with the product or brand while a companion demonstration explains how the product works. Show people where to find the answer to their next reasonable question. Keep essential information accessible even when they watch the campaign out of order.

## Make the product necessary to the idea

For a story about a product, imagine removing it. Would the same plot and conclusion work unchanged for an unrelated offering? If so, identify the actual capability that should change what happens. Mood can support the argument, but you still need a connection between the product and the events of the story.

For a story about a brand, that connection may be a documented commitment or practice. There is no need to force a product demonstration into it. Cultural prestige or the presence of admirable people does not prove capabilities those people never endorsed.

State what the audience does: what do they choose, make, understand, or accomplish? Show a credible role for the tool in that action. Avoid language that attributes the person's skill entirely to the tool or treats their existing practice as worthless.

## Build scenes that change understanding

For a substantial sequence, consider what the audience knows or feels before it begins, what they encounter, and what changes by the end. A reveal can resolve an uncertainty or create a memorable first encounter. A pause can give people time to absorb an idea. Build anticipation only to the extent that the reveal justifies it. Do not delay identifying the product just to imitate a theatrical launch. For each dramatic moment, name what the viewer comes away knowing about the product, whether that is its form, scale, behaviour or what it is like to use. If removing the spectacle would lose nothing about the product, rework or cut it.

Alternate explanation and evidence when that helps people understand. Repetition can make a promise recognisable, sustain a mood or give the ending a sense of return. It needs a purpose in the story, but it need not introduce a new fact each time. Do not design to achieve audience applause, a three-part structure, or a fixed number of reveals.

A live demonstration needs a reliable starting state, visible action and result, honestly stated prerequisites, and a way to recover if it fails. Rehearse transitions as well as the successful action. If you use a prerecorded segment, present it honestly. Do not make people wait on a self-paced page to reproduce the timing of a stage presentation.

## Develop the execution in its actual medium

Work out the argument in plain language while exploring words, imagery, sound and timing together in a rough storyboard. An image or a line can guide the exploration. Check what it implies against the product facts, and judge whether the transitions make sense without an explanation from the creator.

Review how an approved concept works in each format; concept approval does not approve every execution. When making a shorter film, rebuild it around the meaning that must survive. Cutting equal amounts from every scene can destroy its logic. Check names and headlines beside the actual image, in the intended layout, and aloud when spoken.

For recurring work, keep the central premise recognizable while changing the situation or evidence. Agree on the installment's subject before writing variants. Compare how well the alternatives serve that subject and reflect the product facts. Novelty and production polish alone are not enough to judge them.

## Check the metaphor from the audience's position

When the story depends on symbolism, record what you intend it to mean and at least one plausible conflicting interpretation. Ask:

- Who acts, who benefits, and who loses something?
- What is being replaced, erased, mocked, or made obsolete?
- Does the scene diminish the work or identity of the people it hopes to serve?
- Do cultural references, casting, humor, or music carry the same meaning in the intended market?
- Does the literal product claim remain accurate if the emotional framing is removed?

These questions can help you improve the idea, but cannot guarantee universal approval. Where possible, ask intended viewers to explain what happened and what they think the product promises before revealing your intended interpretation. Record disagreement. Do not use internal enthusiasm or executive approval as a substitute for this audience evidence.

## Keep research and conclusions separate

When studying work you admire, look for accounts of its development and substantial criticism as well as the finished work. Label what each source can establish:

- An artifact from the time shows what was communicated.
- A participant's account reports the decisions and intentions they remember; disputes about credit may remain unresolved.
- An independent analysis offers an interpretation that may reveal tensions the creators did not discuss.
- Audience research establishes responses within the limits of its sample and method.
- Commercial results need separate evidence before you attribute them to a narrative choice.

Adapt useful decisions to the current product's circumstances. Do not reproduce the source's slogan, plot, visual identity, or presumed formula. Record why a proposed treatment fits this audience and what evidence would cause you to change it.
