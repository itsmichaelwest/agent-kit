# Writing

Choose something worth saying about the product: an observation the reader recognises, a useful capability, a satisfying action or a detail worth noticing. Develop that idea in the product's own terms. Your choice of idea and how you express it give the copy personality. Not every sentence needs to sound clever.

Compose the headline, image and explanation together as one scene. The headline can invite, surprise or interpret. The image can establish the object or show the result, while the prose explains what changes and why it matters. Each part can contribute without repeating the whole claim.

## Know what the promise means

For an important claim, write down what the product does, why that is useful and any condition that changes the promise. Note which demonstration or evidence supports it. An expressive line or image may come first; check what it implies before adopting it. These working notes do not have to determine the display order or final wording.

For example, downloading saved itineraries for offline reading supports “Download a saved itinerary while connected, then read it without a connection.” It does not establish offline navigation, live updates or access to unsaved trips.

The supplied facts define the product you are presenting, including an approved fictional design. Describe those capabilities directly. When the brief omits a fact, you do not know it. That omission does not establish that the product lacks the capability or that its designers have not decided it. Omit claims that depend on the missing fact, or ask the user to resolve the gap when it is central to the task. Do not make up a benchmark, customer story, quotation, personal experience or colourful detail to sound authentic.

Preserve the meaning of specifications, comparisons and conditions. Include the units and baseline needed to understand a number. Put conditions that affect the promise near the claim: availability, compatibility, prerequisites, account tier or usage limits. A footnote may contain a method or longer explanation, but it must not reverse the natural reading of the headline. When actual future capabilities belong in the requested story, state that they are future capabilities.

## Keep the product and its presentation distinct

Write about what the audience is considering: the product, how they use it and what it enables or changes. Product engineering can contribute to the story when it explains an advantage. The implementation of the marketing website is a different subject.

Keep information about asset origins, generated sample data, prototype limitations, missing inputs and development decisions in the project handoff. Do not turn it into public copy about how the page was built, what its simulation does not do, or which browser permissions it does not request. An FAQ about whether a marketing demo records, uploads or saves anything generally answers the author's concern rather than a buyer's product question. Include privacy copy when it describes an established property of the product that matters to its audience.

For a concept study, present the intended finished product defined by the brief. The review context already establishes that the work is a study, so do not repeatedly label images, scenes or the footer as conceptual or illustrative. Missing brief details do not belong in a public “what is settled” or roadmap section unless that is the requested subject.

Use the presentation's design to make clear what it demonstrates. A clear invitation to explore a control, followed by a visible example of its effect, usually identifies an illustration without a disclaimer paragraph. Do not label an illustrative action as a real purchase, save or export. If a specific result could reasonably be mistaken for measured performance or an actual transaction, change how you present it or clarify that result at the point of use. When the work is published to real buyers and shows a concept or an offer that is not yet available, give them the context needed to understand what is actually offered. Keep that clarification where it is needed; avoid describing prototype mechanics throughout the page.

## Start with the thought

Ask what the reader should notice here that they did not know before. It may be a physical detail, an unfamiliar mechanism, a useful consequence or a clearer view of a familiar situation. Choose the most relevant idea instead of combining every possible benefit.

Try explaining the idea to an interested person in one or two ordinary sentences. Then compose the scene. The visual may make half the explanation unnecessary. A short headline might name the benefit while a paragraph makes it credible. Preserve the useful idea as the wording becomes more expressive.

Do not force or invent content to make a line sound rhythmic. Keep phrasing only when its meaning belongs to the product and scene. Paired fragments, dramatic contrasts, rhetorical questions and lists of three verbs can make a line sound like a slogan even when it says nothing about the product. Use such a form when the idea it carries belongs to this product, and write it as the headline or sentence itself, not as a motto added above or beside it. If a line could accompany almost any product, changing its adjectives will not make it specific.

A scene may create a feeling without introducing a new specification. Make that feeling recognisable through the product, situation and visual. Mottos that announce freedom, possibility, focus or craft rarely make that connection by themselves.

## Let expression earn its place

Wit works when its second meaning reveals something true. A surprising turn of phrase can make a useful consequence memorable. Repetition can connect two related actions, and a short phrase can give an image a new interpretation. The reader should recognise the connection without needing the author to defend it.

Compare the direct version with the expressive one. Keep the expressive line when it clarifies the idea, engages the reader more or fits the image better. Keep the direct line when it is clearer and stronger. A literal feature heading can be finished copy.

A small verbal flourish can give a chapter its character. The supporting paragraph usually benefits from ordinary language. Avoid extending the same metaphor through the heading, explanation, caption and button, or ending every explanation with another punchline. Do not borrow a recognised slogan's structure and substitute the product noun.

Use fragments or full sentences according to the meaning you need to express. Rewrite a pun that obscures the feature, an opposition that invents a false problem, or a phrase made awkward to achieve symmetry. For international audiences, preserve the idea when translating; you can drop an English word game.

## Give explanation room to explain

Use the prose to develop the headline's idea. Name the relevant action, mechanism or consequence in familiar language. A reader may need to know what happens, when it helps and what they can do next. Supply the parts that matter to this scene. You do not need to follow that sequence in every paragraph.

Use concrete nouns and active verbs. Technical terms are appropriate when the audience needs them; explain why they matter instead of replacing them with vague praise. A longer sentence can connect cause and effect more naturally than several emphatic fragments. Useful detail may need more than one line.

Answer a practical question near the capability when it resolves a real concern about the product: reversing a change, choosing an option, continuing a task or meeting a requirement. Do not invent an objection to create a reassuring answer. If the supplied facts do not support an FAQ with useful new answers, omit the FAQ.

An additional sentence should develop the idea. Do not add one merely to fill a visual treatment, or split a simple statement into several lines to make it appear more substantial.

## Start without supporting ornament

Omit eyebrows by default. These are the small category labels placed above headlines. Use one only when it resolves a specific ambiguity or provides necessary orientation in a complex sequence. A section that already clearly presents its topic does not need an announcement. A product name already established in navigation does not need to return as a decorative label. Numbering ordinary chapters does not make their order meaningful.

Leaving out the label does not remove the need to say what the product is. If the opening image could be mistaken for something else, let the headline or first sentence identify the product in ordinary words, close to the main promise.

Useful labels still belong on controls, navigation and diagrams. A number or letter on an image can connect a visible part to its explanation. At the actual viewing size, the reader must be able to see which part the label identifies. These functions do not justify a separate label above the whole scene.

Add a caption when it helps the reader notice or understand a detail they would otherwise miss. Explain why the detail matters instead of describing an obvious image or how it was made. Let an image stand alone when it does the job. If comparable images receive different caption treatments, make sure there is a consistent reason for the difference.

Apply the same judgment at the edges of the page or slide. A footer should offer useful navigation, required information or an intentional close. It does not need an extra maxim. An action needs a caption only when its consequence would otherwise be unclear. Do not invent a generic guide, download or signup just to fill the closing composition.

Removing text can improve pacing and make priorities clearer without flattening the story. After deleting a label, you can leave the space, strengthen the image or simplify the composition. You do not need to replace it with another form of decoration.

## Establish a recognisable voice

Use the user's writing samples or established voice when available. Look at sentence length, everyday or specialist vocabulary, direct address, punctuation, use of wordplay and confidence of claims. Without a sample, choose a few practical characteristics appropriate to the audience. “Calm and capable,” for example, might mean familiar verbs, useful explanations and occasional contractions.

Keep the voice recognisable while adjusting the tone to the situation. An opening may be expansive. Compatibility needs precision, and an error needs a clear recovery action. Warmth can come from noticing a real question and answering it thoughtfully. Artificial informality, deliberate mistakes, fake uncertainty and invented personal asides do not make copy human.

## Revise for meaning

These original examples depend on the stated facts. Use them to understand the editorial decisions; do not reuse them as headlines.

| Material | Weak direction | Revision and reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Saved itineraries can be read offline after download | A promise of seamless travel without limits | Say what can be downloaded and read. Preserve the prerequisite; do not imply offline navigation. |
| A tool restores saved revisions | “Never lose a thought again.” | Explain returning to a saved version. The real capability is useful without a guarantee that nothing can be lost. |
| A lamp's head can turn toward a work surface | An abstract promise about bringing ideas to light | Show the adjustment and describe directing the light where it is needed. The physical action gives the scene its meaning. |
| A tier allows five projects | “Limitless possibilities for ambitious teams.” | “Keep up to five projects in one workspace.” The actual capacity helps the reader choose. |

When a line feels forced, identify the useful idea underneath it. Rewrite that idea in natural speech before deciding whether to make the wording more expressive. Deleting fashionable words will not supply a missing idea.

## Write useful interactions

Control labels should let someone predict the result. Use the product's terms consistently in visible labels, selected states and accessible names. State the actual commitment or destination. Use buy, download, start a trial or register interest only when that action exists. A useful next step may stay within the page.

Keep visible controls concise and give icon-only or repeated controls enough accessible context to identify their target. “Play” and “Pause” must track the current action. Expose selection through control semantics; colour alone is insufficient. A comparison's baseline and result must update together. Do not say “Rotate” when a control only swaps two fixed images.

A disclosure should name the actual question or topic. Loading and unavailable states need accurate, useful copy, with an alternative where one exists. Essential qualifications must remain available outside optional disclosures. Use `product-web-design` for website component behaviour, or [Slide decks](slide-decks.md) for presentation controls.

Write instructions that help the reader use the scene. Do not use them to explain the code, list everything the prototype cannot do or turn every control into a conversion pitch.

## Read the whole piece

Read the opening, chapter titles and closing with the main images. Then read the prose aloud. Listen for mannerisms that accumulate: every heading uses two fragments, every paragraph ends in a slogan, false contrasts repeat, or the same feature has several names. Keep deliberate repetition that develops an idea; remove repetition caused by a template.

Review the complete public story for text or scenes you can remove. For each supporting line, caption, heading continuation or repeated scene, consider what the reader would lose if it disappeared. Remove or combine it when the surrounding words and imagery already supply its meaning. For example, after a close-up explains a physical control, a demonstration can show its effect without another caption naming the control and a later inventory explaining it again. Keep a useful reference to part of an image, an instruction needed to explore, a qualification that affects the claim or a deliberate return that gives the story closure. The pass removes redundant text. Leave the expressive headlines, strong imagery, deliberate pauses and composed closing scene intact. Make an editorial judgment; do not impose a word limit or ban repetition.

Check the actual layout, including narrow screens and expanded states. A good line can become awkward when wrapped, detached from its image or repeated beside several controls. Captions that work individually can still make a page feel crowded or overexplained when shown together.

Before delivery, ask:

- Does each explanation develop the thought rather than paraphrase its headline?
- Does the full sequence progress, or explain the same capability again in a new component?
- Does any eyebrow resolve a real ambiguity that the headline, image or navigation cannot resolve?
- Do captions, footers and FAQ answers concern something the product's audience needs?
- Is any line explaining the prototype or the author's uncertainty instead of the product?
- Did polishing invent a capability, guarantee or use case? Did shortening remove a material condition?
- Does the full sequence feel like someone explaining this particular product, with room for both expression and ordinary language?

Punctuation, fragments and particular words are not reliable evidence of authorship. Judge their meaning and cumulative effect. For a copy task, deliver the requested copy with unresolved factual gaps identified separately. For a working page or deck, keep production notes in the handoff and report only checks actually performed.
