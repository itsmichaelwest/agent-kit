# Product storytelling

Give people a reason to notice the product and understand why it matters. Make the story memorable through words and images that suit this product. Support the promises you ask the audience to believe.

Use this skill to develop the argument, write the copy, choose evidence, decide what the imagery should convey and order the scenes. Each scene should contribute to the story and build on what the audience has already understood. For website composition, typography, responsive layout, reusable web patterns and frontend execution, use `product-web-design` alongside this skill when available. Copy and deck tasks can use this guide without a web implementation guide. Honour explicitly requested guides in their assigned roles.

## Editorial defaults

- Start without eyebrows, the small category labels above headlines. If the reader could mistake what the product is, say so in the headline or opening sentence. Add an eyebrow only when the reader would still mistake the subject or would lose their place in a complex sequence. Repeating the product name, announcing an obvious topic or adding a decorative section number does not help with either problem. Keep useful navigation, control labels and annotations that identify something in an image.
- Describe the product and what it makes possible. Put information about asset sources, missing brief details and the marketing prototype's implementation in the handoff. Keep it out of public captions, footers and FAQ answers. For a concept study, present the supplied design as the intended finished product. Preserve real conditions on claims, and do not invent availability or working transactions.
- Start the writing from a specific observation, capability or consequence. Clipped fragments, forced contrasts and wordplay cannot make a generic thought specific. An expressive headline can draw attention; ordinary sentences can explain what is useful.
- Once the audience understands an idea, develop its consequence, evidence or significance. Avoid explaining the same capability again in the opening, an observation, a demonstration caption and a closing inventory. Return to an idea when recognition or closure helps the story.

## Principles

- Let people see the product's form, behaviour, output or setting. Any of these can invite curiosity before a detailed explanation. Choose an introduction that suits this particular product.
- Compose words and images together. A headline can express a feeling while an image shows what the product is or how it works. Give each part a useful role in the scene; they do not each need to repeat the complete claim.
- Give the central promise enough evidence and room to be understood. Present familiar supporting details more compactly. Choose the story's length according to the reader's interest and need for explanation. Include only the sections that serve that story.
- Connect the promise to recognisable situations, useful explanations and convincing evidence. Anticipation, humour, a pause or a return to the whole product can also develop the experience. A scene can contribute without introducing another specification.
- Use specific observations, product states, imagery and language to give the story character. Replace interchangeable praise with something that belongs to this product. Preserve the established voice and identity while making original work.
- Adapt the argument to each format. A webpage, speaker-led deck and self-paced presentation can change the sequence, amount of detail and audience's control while sharing the same argument. Keep the product facts and any qualifications that affect the promise intact.

Use these principles to make decisions, with freedom to choose the plot. The work still needs truthful representation, readable content, usable controls and appropriate asset rights. A gap in the brief tells you that information is missing; it does not establish that the product is unfinished. Keep working notes outside the audience's story.

## How to work

Start with the user's task and existing material. Use only the parts of this workflow needed for the revision.

1. Find the argument and the feeling it should convey. Use [Narrative strategy](references/narrative-strategy.md) for the audience's situation, the main promise and what people need to understand before each claim. Use [Story development](references/story-development.md) for campaign premises, launch scripts and adapting the narrative.
2. Choose what deserves attention with [Evidence and hierarchy](references/evidence-and-hierarchy.md). An invitation, explanation, proof and optional detail serve different purposes. Use them where needed; the story does not have to pass through each one in order.
3. Develop words and scenes together. Read [Writing](references/writing.md) before drafting public copy. Use [Identity](references/identity.md) and [Visual direction](references/visual-direction.md) for campaign character, image purpose and scene planning. Inspect the actual assets and preserve the user's established direction.
4. Adapt to the deliverable. Use [Slide decks and keynotes](references/slide-decks.md) for presented keynotes and for static and interactive presentations. For website design or implementation, carry the product facts, copy intent and scene purposes into `product-web-design`. Frontend work should support the established argument.
5. Review the complete public story. Read the copy with its images as the audience will see it. Make the [subtraction pass](references/writing.md#read-the-whole-piece) to check the writing even when the layout looks good and the implementation works.

## Review questions

- Does the story give people a clear reason to care about this particular product?
- Does the sequence develop the argument with appropriate evidence and useful changes of emphasis?
- Does each passage or supporting line add meaning, help the reader find their place or make an intentional expressive connection?
- Would removing a repeated explanation or recap make the story clearer without losing something the audience needs?
- Is it clear which image detail an annotation identifies and which action a control label describes?
- Do the promise, imagery and qualifications agree across the requested formats?

Report story findings separately from visual execution and operation, with detail appropriate to the task. A working prototype does not establish good writing or audience acceptance. Put production notes and unresolved gaps in the brief in the handoff.
