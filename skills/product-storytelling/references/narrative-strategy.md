# Narrative strategy

A product story gives the audience a reason to care, a reason to believe, and enough information to decide what to do. Each chapter should develop the same central promise. Polishing the visuals will not resolve a promise that changes halfway through the presentation.

## Establish the product truth

Begin with the supplied facts, audience, and purpose. Separate what you know from what you infer. Use the following questions to work through the brief. You do not need to make it a formal document:

| Decision | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Product | What is it, and which capabilities are established by evidence or the approved design brief? |
| Audience situation | What is the person trying to do, and what do they already understand? |
| Relevant tension | What difficulty, uncertainty, tradeoff, or aspiration makes the product matter? |
| Distinction | What gives this product a credible role among the available alternatives? |
| Evidence | What can the audience inspect, try, compare, or verify? |
| Boundaries | Which conditions, dependencies, and limitations affect the promise? |
| Remaining doubt | What could still stop a reader from making a sound decision, and where will the story answer it? |
| Decision | What should the audience be able to decide after this story? |

For a concept, tell the story of the approved design. Missing details in the brief leave questions for the author. They do not establish product limitations or a future roadmap. Resolve those questions when the central argument depends on them. Otherwise, leave out claims that depend on the missing details. Follow [Writing](writing.md#know-what-the-promise-means) to distinguish internal notes from qualifications the audience needs to see.

A tension can be small and specific. Someone may want easier physical handling, more control over an output, compatibility with existing work, or a familiar experience in a new form. Do not invent a crisis, portray ordinary life as broken, or claim that customers feel something without evidence.

Positioning describes the product's role for this audience. Use it to choose which capabilities lead and which remain supporting detail. Adjectives such as premium, powerful, elegant, or innovative cannot define that role on their own or tell you how to order the chapters.

If the brief calls the product premium, identify what supports that expectation: useful precision, distinctive output, material quality, durability, handling, service, or another quality you can demonstrate. A high price or dramatic presentation alone does not explain the claim. Use the evidence you have, and do not imply craftsmanship, exclusivity, or heritage that the product cannot substantiate.

## Choose the governing promise

Write one working sentence that states the useful change the product offers to this audience. Use it to keep the draft consistent. It need not become the headline.

For example: “This device lets a field technician record readings on site and bring them into the office workflow without retyping them.” Every noun and capability in that sentence needs support. The story can include handling, recording, transfer, and compatibility because they contribute to that outcome. A section about unrelated entertainment features would need a separate reason to belong.

You can keep one central promise while showing that the product has several uses. Group secondary capabilities under the jobs they support, and make their relative importance visible. If the product serves different audiences, provide distinct paths or explain when you move from one audience to another.

For each major claim, connect the mechanism, the proof someone can inspect, and the useful consequence.

Use these connections to check the reasoning behind the claim. Choose the display order to suit the presentation: you may open with the consequence, show proof, then explain the mechanism. A technically skeptical audience may need the mechanism earlier. A broad promise is easier to believe once the reader has seen something it rests on, so let a demonstration carry the larger ambition when you can. Whatever order you choose, make the relationship clear.

A specification does not always prove the desired outcome. A processor figure alone does not demonstrate a smoother editing task; a material name alone does not establish durability. Support the actual claim with a representative workflow, a measurement with its conditions and limits stated, a visible result, or other suitable evidence. State when the evidence is only a demonstration rather than a controlled comparison.

Choose feelings that could credibly follow from the outcome. Better visibility might support confidence; reduced routine effort might support relief; control over a creative process might support satisfaction. These are creative choices, not measured psychological effects. Avoid promising a changed identity or life when the evidence establishes a modest practical improvement.

## Choose a narrative mode

Select the approach that addresses the audience's main uncertainty. You can combine approaches, but choose one to organize the story.

| Mode | What the audience needs to understand | A useful development | Common mismatch |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Unfamiliar form or category | What it is and why the form matters | Show the object or behavior, demonstrate a recognizable use, then explain the design | Beginning with an unexplained specification list |
| Creative instrument | What it helps people make and how it feels to use | Show credible output, connect controls to creative choices, explain workflow and handling | Treating a performance number as proof of artistry |
| Engineered problem-solving | Whether a mechanism improves a particular task | Show the friction, demonstrate the result, explain the mechanism, address maintenance | Adding abstract lifestyle language that obscures the task |
| Familiar experience with new capability | What is preserved and what becomes possible | Establish the valued experience, show the new behavior, explain the tradeoffs | Celebrating feature volume while promising restraint |
| Professional upgrade | Whether the difference matters for existing work | Name the baseline, show relevant changes, demonstrate a workload, explain migration | Presenting every improvement as equally important |
| Everyday adoption | Whether the product will fit ordinary routines | Show familiar activities, introduce capabilities in context, answer setup and ownership questions | Assuming category knowledge or demanding technical interest too early |
| Software release | What changes for current users and who can use it | Lead with the meaningful change, show workflows, group supporting updates, state applicability | Using a checkout narrative when the decision is compatibility or adoption |

These options do not define market segments or prescribe a launch formula. A small product may need only a clear promise, one good demonstration, and an honest explanation of fit.

## Build chapter dependencies

For each proposed chapter, note the question it answers, the evidence it uses, and what the reader needs to understand first. Arrange the chapters so that explanations arrive before they are needed.

Give each transition a reason. You might move from the result to its mechanism, from a capability to a use, from a technical improvement to a comparison, or from interest to an ownership question. Changing the background color alone does not make that connection.

Before keeping a chapter, ask what it helps the reader understand, become curious about, identify with, notice, or decide. A pause or return can give an established idea more force. Merge repetition that adds neither meaning nor rhythm. Put technical detail that matters to only some readers on a clear path they can choose to explore. Keep information that changes the purchasing decision accessible in the main story.

Give each chapter the space it needs. A striking result may need little copy. A tradeoff, workflow, or new category may need more explanation. Do not give every feature the same headline, paragraph, and card treatment just to maintain symmetry.

Before polishing the whole story, write it in plain language as though you were demonstrating the product to the intended reader in person. State what matters, show how it works, and answer the next reasonable question. Read it aloud. If you need a strained transition or an unexplained change of subject to connect sections, revise the outline before adding slogans.

## Align words, imagery, and motion

When a scene makes or implies a factual claim, identify what the audience can see and what you ask them to infer. Keep that inference reasonable and limited. An expressive passage can suggest a feeling or possibility without demonstrating how the product works. Assess any facts it implies separately.

- A close-up of a control can explain its position or construction. It does not establish that an entire workflow is faster.
- An interface recording can demonstrate a supported action. Avoid edits that imply instantaneous processing or skipped setup unless that is how the product behaves.
- An exploded view can clarify a mechanism. Show how the parts contribute to the benefit; complexity alone does not explain it.
- A scene of use can establish context and scale. It should portray a plausible use with the actual product capabilities.
- A comparison needs matched conditions and a named baseline. Do not let framing, timing, or unequal content manufacture the difference.

Motion can reveal a physical transformation, follow an input to its result, or move attention to a relevant detail. The caption and current visual state should describe the same moment. If the page freezes on a poster frame or a person skips ahead, the core claim should still make sense.

Let the imagery and pace support the feeling you want to convey. A story about careful control should give people time to inspect the result. A story about convenience should not force a long interaction before revealing basic information. Judge whether these choices are consistent with the story. A particular mood does not require a specific color or animation speed.

## Keep the story consistent across formats

Keep the same promise, product facts, conditions, limitations, and vocabulary across the launch pitch, demonstration, and product page. Change the amount of detail and sequence to suit the format.

| Format | Main responsibility |
| --- | --- |
| Short launch pitch | Establish what is new, why it matters, and one credible reason to believe it |
| Demonstration | Show the mechanism or workflow and make its result observable; explain required setup and conditions |
| Product page | Let readers explore at their own pace, compare relevant options, check fit, and take the next action |
| Slide deck | Develop the argument through distinct visual steps; supply explanation through the presenter or on-slide content according to delivery mode |
| Technical or support detail | Resolve precise questions about compatibility, setup, operation, and limits |

A shorter pitch can omit secondary detail, but should not make a broader promise. A product page can add depth without introducing a different reason to want the product. The demonstration should prove the promise people have just heard. Do not substitute a claim that is more convenient to demonstrate.

For the relationship between brand beliefs, campaign premises, launch propositions, and their execution, use [Story development](story-development.md). You can preserve the meaning while changing the script for each medium.

Preserve essential qualifications when reusing material. A cropped clip or abbreviated caption may lose the condition that made the original claim accurate. Recheck the final format. Approval of the source copy does not guarantee that it remains correct in every context.

For keynotes and for speaker-led, self-paced, static, and interactive presentations, use [Slide decks and keynotes](slide-decks.md), including its export checks. A deck can preserve the story while dividing its chapters into different-sized parts.

## Worked example

Consider a fictional coffee grinder. The verified facts are: a stepped grind-size dial, a built-in scale that shows the weight of ground coffee, and a burr set that lifts out for cleaning. There is no evidence for better-tasting coffee, quieter operation, more consistent grinding than a competitor, or app connectivity.

An inconsistent outline might promise café-quality coffee every morning, show a dramatic macro of the burrs, celebrate an invented smart brewing profile, then end with a list of dimensions. The opening promises an outcome the evidence cannot establish. The burr close-up does not demonstrate taste, and the final specifications have no clear relationship to the story.

A coherent alternative is:

1. Introduce a grinder for people who adjust and weigh their coffee at home.
2. Show a person turning the dial, with grounds from two settings side by side. Explain how this gives them control and what changes, without claiming better flavor.
3. Show the routine: grind into the cup while the scale shows the weight, then lift out the burrs to clean them. Keep real steps visible.
4. Help readers judge fit with supported capacity, size, and power requirements when those facts are available and relevant. Keep missing inputs in working notes; do not turn them into a chapter about an undecided product.
5. Offer the appropriate next step according to the brief: inspect details, try a demonstration, or purchase.

The second outline is only one plausible treatment. It could begin with the morning routine or with the physical control. It works because the evidence develops a supported promise; it does not need five sections.

## Review coherence

Review the story's argument before its layout:

- Can a reader state the governing promise without using a generic quality adjective?
- Does each major scene develop that promise or help the reader make a relevant decision?
- Does the mechanism plausibly connect to the claimed consequence?
- Does the evidence support the exact claim, or only a related one?
- Do the words, imagery, and interaction describe the same product state?
- Are audience changes and technical prerequisites introduced clearly?
- Do limitations, subscriptions, accessories, or compatibility change the promise?
- Does the tone remain appropriate when the subject shifts to risk or cost?
- Is the closing action a reasonable next step given what the story has established?

If an attractive chapter weakens the overall promise, change its role, move it to a secondary path, or remove it. Assess clarity and consistency directly. When intended readers are available, show them the opening and the main transitions, then ask what they understood and which evidence persuaded them. Asking whether it feels premium tells you little. Claims about conversion, trust, or emotional response require separate research or measurement.
