# Product web design

Build a website that makes the product interesting and understandable. Choose imagery and composition from its form, behaviour or output. Use typography, space and interaction to draw attention, help people inspect the product and offer a useful next step.

Use credible product imagery and make the main idea easy to find. Keep surrounding controls visually secondary. Give important details room to be seen, and present supporting features more compactly. Colour, texture, rounded frames, expressive type and dramatic reveals can all work within this approach. Choose fonts, colours and components for how they work together; the guide does not prescribe a set.

## Ownership

Use this skill for the website's visual design and execution. It covers how to present the product, arrange words and images, use type and surfaces, pace the page, adapt to different screens and build the controls people use to explore. Frontend precision helps those choices work as intended.

Preserve the established product argument, factual boundaries and voice. Use `product-storytelling` when positioning, narrative or substantial copy needs development. Keep an approved story unless the task calls for revising it. Decks and general application interfaces do not need this website workflow. Honour explicitly named guides, and bring in a specialist for a specific question the task raises. Avoid combining several complete design workflows into one set of stylistic requirements.

## Design principles

- Give product content the leading role. A whole object, a useful crop, an actual screen state or a visible result can be the focus. Show it at a size where the important detail is legible. A tall screen may fit beside copy; another product may need a broad, open area. Choose its size from the content, without requiring it to fill a fixed share of the viewport. An opening can first establish the place or moment the product serves, with the product appearing in the same scene. Overlap, a shared edge or a continuing colour can make the connection visible. The setting should belong to this product; generic atmosphere delays the introduction.
- Compose words and imagery together. Decide what people should notice first, what becomes clear next and what each part adds. Large type and large media can work together without competing. Start without eyebrows above headlines and without the product name repeated as a label. Add an eyebrow, strapline or caption only when it resolves an ambiguity or adds meaning the scene lacks; available space alone is not a reason. Keep useful labels and annotations that explain something visible.
- Make the hierarchy clear. Give the main thought, explanation and controls distinct but related emphasis. Limit competing treatments so people can scan the main idea and choose what to inspect more closely. Every line does not need equal attention.
- Keep enough consistency for people to follow changes. Repeat framing and alignment when the product content is what changes. Adjust scale, density, viewpoint or surface when the reader's task changes. A large demonstration and a compact feature group can share the same visual style. Reserve dramatic chapters for features that benefit from them.
- Use space to group related parts, separate thoughts and give the product time to be noticed. Look at what the audience can see during a pause. Reduce an empty interval if it adds no anticipation, emphasis or rest. Vary the density where the content calls for it instead of making every scene a dense card or an oversized display.
- Give familiar forms a purpose in this product's story. Device frames, cards, icons, gradients, system typography and quiet neutrals remain available. Use real product states, deliberate crops, selected content and a consistent atmosphere to make them specific. Adapt useful relationships from references into original work. Preserve the distinction between learning from a composition and copying its marks, artwork or distinctive signatures.
- Use interaction to reveal something worth exploring. A selector can show a difference, a close-up can identify a detail, and a short sequence can explain how something works. Keep the product and its result more prominent than the controls. Use a static presentation when it explains the idea better.
- Preserve the connections between content and controls as the presentation changes. Narrow screens, enlarged text, keyboard input and reduced motion may need different arrangements. Keep the subject, explanation, controls and selected states clearly associated. Check that connection when adapting a desktop layout or replacing a transition with a still.

## Work from the material

Read the brief, approved copy and available assets before choosing a layout. When references are supplied, inspect the relevant scenes at different sizes and in their interactive states. Record what you see in the rendered page separately from behaviour described by its source. Learn from how the parts work together; the reference's individual design values are not mandatory.

Decide which image introduces the product, what the main demonstrations need to show and which features can be presented compactly. Choose a visual character that supports the product. Explore alternatives when an important choice remains unresolved, and preserve a direction the user has endorsed.

Develop representative scenes with the actual words and media they will use. Judge the whole arrangement before polishing individual effects. If imagery is missing, use an appropriate authorised production method and keep the product's identity consistent across assets. A concept render illustrates a design; measured performance needs separate evidence. Put information about how assets were produced in the handoff, except where a specific public claim needs clarification.

## Review the result

Read the public story with its imagery, then inspect the rendered page at sizes that test the layout. Review the writing separately from alignment and interaction checks. A technically correct scene can still repeat an idea the reader already understands.

Ask:

- Does the product draw attention at the scale and crop the scene needs?
- Do type, imagery and space establish a clear reading path, with enough detail for closer inspection?
- Do repeated frames create continuity while changes in density reflect changes in importance?
- Do captions or annotations reveal useful meaning, or repeat information already obvious nearby?
- Do visible edges and baselines line up as intended? Are controls and image callouts clear at the relevant widths?
- Can people explore, leave and return without losing state or orientation?
- Do the responsive and reduced-motion versions preserve the important relationships between content, controls and states?

Compare the result with the supplied references at equivalent viewing sizes. Explain how composition and behaviour compare; matching colours or corner radii alone says little about the result. Report visual craft, writing and operation separately. A visual review does not establish accessibility: check focus order, accessible names, contrast and text enlargement against WCAG, and use an accessibility specialist where one is available. State what you rendered or exercised and what remains unverified. Source checks and visual similarity do not establish audience acceptance or business results.
