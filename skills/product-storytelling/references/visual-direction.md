# Visual direction

Art direction combines the subject, words, imagery and pace to create a particular experience. It should make the product compelling to look at and worth understanding. Use [Identity](identity.md) to define its character and [Evidence and hierarchy](evidence-and-hierarchy.md) to decide how much attention and support each idea needs. This reference covers planning scenes and choosing imagery. Use `product-web-design` when turning that direction into a website.

## Decide what the encounter should do

A striking image can establish the product's presence before explaining a feature. A demonstration can make an ordinary benefit tangible, while a small, precise caption can reward closer reading. Decide how you want the viewer to engage with each moment, then choose how to present it.

Make the visual priority clear, relate the words to the images, and choose details that belong to this product. Understated framing can give a vivid image more force. Dramatic typography can briefly lead a scene. Judge either approach by what it makes someone notice and how it fits the surrounding story; quietness or drama alone does not make a composition work.

When a draft is rejected, identify whether the problem is the argument, assets, composition or execution. A better font cannot repair irrelevant imagery, and more imagery cannot repair a promise with no substance. Compare alternative scenes when the direction remains uncertain. Preserve a direction the user has already endorsed.

## Develop scenes at the needed level of detail

For a substantial new piece, a loose scene list or storyboard can make the argument clear before production becomes expensive. Record the important decisions. You do not need to fill out a form for every section:

- The scene's purpose: invitation, explanation, demonstration, emphasis, pause or decision support.
- What the viewer notices first and what becomes apparent on closer inspection.
- What the image conveys and what the words add.
- The real asset or proposed treatment, and any implied factual claim that needs support.
- What must remain legible when the scene changes size or format.

Sketch, write and inspect assets together. Revise the scene list if an actual image or line suggests a stronger composition. A small revision does not need a new storyboard.

Check the concrete words in the main promise and headlines against the scenes. A noun such as a place, a period, a measurement or an action should have something the reader can see, or a clear reason it needs no image. If showing a word would mean inventing an image the facts do not support, move the word into the explanation or remove it. Check, too, that each headline claims no more than its scene and explanation support.

Check concrete promises against available evidence. A metaphor can suggest an idea visually without illustrating it literally. A stated capability, comparison or result needs appropriate support. The [evidence guide](evidence-and-hierarchy.md) explains how to distinguish these uses.

## Direct the image for its purpose

| Image role | Useful choices | What to protect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Object or material | A whole-object view to establish presence; a close crop to suggest how it feels; light that reveals or dramatizes form | Consistent geometry and finish; no accidental ambiguity about what the product is |
| In use | A gesture, activity or setting that makes the product's scale or consequence tangible | Plausible use and a credible relationship between person, product and context |
| Interface | A complete state for context; a closer crop for an action; a sequence for a change | Faithful UI, coherent content and enough readable detail to understand the intended point |
| Output | Work shown large enough to see the quality that matters to the scene | A truthful relationship to what the product can produce |
| Atmospheric or conceptual | An image, illustration or motif that expresses the campaign's idea | A meaningful connection to the story and no misleading implication of measured capability |

A seamless backdrop and soft light can reveal an object's shape clearly. Hard light, a coloured set, contextual props or a staged portrait can express a different character. Choose and control the treatment for the scene. No single lighting recipe defines quality. Consistency matters most when viewers are meant to compare states; other scenes can deliberately change viewpoint or mood.

A sequence of screen states can tell a complete software story by developing the content, actions and consequences. Do not add lifestyle photography to satisfy a variety quota. Device frames can establish platform or scale, but keep the relevant screen detail large enough to be useful. A wireframe is appropriate when it is the product's actual output or an openly identified design study. It is a poor substitute when the scene claims to demonstrate a finished interface.

## Select or produce assets

Inspect supplied photography, renders, recordings and screenshots before settling the composition. Preserve their aspect ratio and relevant product details. Use crops and framing to focus attention without implying a different workflow or configuration.

When scenes depicting the same workflow or comparison need designed interface content or sample data, build one coherent set with the same places, dates, people and values. Derive those scenes from that set. Different use cases may use distinct examples. Keep screens, controls and captions in agreement, so a caption can direct attention to a value the reader can find in the image.

Choose a production method for missing assets. Capture an actual interface when available. Build a faithful recreation only when appropriate, or design a concept UI when the brief permits it. Photography, illustration, diagrams, 3D and image generation have different strengths. A diagram may explain a mechanism better than a photograph. A scene about the product's physical presence may need a convincing view of the object.

For generated concept imagery, define which features of the object must stay consistent and what the image should convey. Establish a useful reference view, then use references to preserve the object's identity across shots. Inspect geometry, controls, finish, text and screen content; asking for consistency in the prompt does not establish it. Vary light and framing deliberately where that helps the scene. Generated imagery illustrates a design. It does not independently verify product performance.

If a necessary asset is unavailable, reconsider the scene or omit it. If the scene must stay, deliver it as an incomplete study and name the missing asset in the handoff, not in public copy. A composition that depends on a placeholder is not finished art direction. A placeholder is useful while composing the scene, but an unrelated decorative substitute does not supply missing evidence. Do not require a placeholder when a truthful diagram or another complete treatment serves the intended purpose.

Judge concept imagery as part of the intended product presentation. How it was produced does not by itself require a visible caption. Use the [writing guidance](writing.md#keep-the-product-and-its-presentation-distinct) to decide whether the audience needs clarification about its status. Keep asset origins and gaps in the authoring material in the handoff.

## Recognise weak composition without banning its ingredients

Use these symptoms to decide what to inspect. They do not establish who or what authored the work. A familiar element can be handled with care, and an unusual element can still lack a purpose.

| Symptom | Better decision |
| --- | --- |
| Generic praise beside an unrelated image | Find an observation and a visual that belong together and to this product |
| Every feature receives the same emphasis | Distinguish major scenes, smaller examples and compact inventory |
| Huge type or empty space has nothing useful to emphasize | Strengthen the scene's content or rebalance its scale and duration |
| Extra words appear only to complete a visual formula | Keep the useful thought and let the composition adapt to it |
| A tiny mock-up makes the claimed detail unreadable | Enlarge, crop or recompose it; a split layout may still be the right arrangement |
| Glows, texture or floating panels could belong to any product | Connect the treatment to the direction or remove it |
| Cards and shadows dominate their contents | Make the surfaces less prominent while preserving useful grouping and authentic UI depth |
| A chart or render implies an unmeasured result | State what it establishes and support or revise the stronger claim |
| An icon, connector or arrow suggests a format, port or transfer method the facts do not establish | Use a neutral symbol or words; a nearby qualification does not cancel what the drawing implies |
| A marker, bullet or step glyph looks like a checkbox, button or other control | Redraw it so only real controls look interactive |
| After the opening, the product disappears and later chapters are text alone | Give each major chapter something to see that supports its point, such as a state, crop or result. Shorten the story when no further evidence exists |
| Every chapter repeats praise without development | Give repetition a role in recognition, comparison or emphasis; combine redundant passages |
| Every chapter changes style to appear creative | Restore continuity so readers can follow changes of content and emphasis |
| Small labels and captions repeat what the scene already communicates | Remove redundant layers; retain annotations that orient, explain or connect meaningfully to the image |
| Repeated caveats become the narrative | Present the supplied design; keep gaps in the authoring material in the handoff and clarify actual claims where needed |
| A closing slogan introduces a new promise | Resolve or recall the established idea and offer an appropriate next step |

## Review the rendered work

View the whole sequence at reduced scale to assess the distribution of visual weight, continuity and rhythm. Then inspect representative scenes at reading size: the opening, a major demonstration, supporting detail and the close where those exist. For a website, include wide, narrow and an intermediate layout. Choose sizes appropriate to the supported viewing context. For a deck, inspect at its expected viewing distance and in the delivered format.

Record conclusions in enough detail for the assignment:

1. Story: the experience has a coherent point of view, and its promises, evidence and qualifications agree.
2. Craft: imagery, typography, line breaks, alignment, space and pacing work together. The important details are visible.
3. Identity: the direction is recognisable and specific, including any deliberate departures from its usual treatment.
4. Operation: the implemented interactions, responsive behaviour, motion alternatives and exports work as checked.

Passing one of these checks does not establish the others. Compare the work with the requested references at equivalent viewing sizes. Explain the relationships you are comparing instead of scoring isolated similarities. Name material gaps and untested conditions. A design study can be successful while remaining unfinished. A functional preview alone does not establish visual quality or audience acceptance.

Fix each problem in the part of the work that causes it. Repeated explanation calls for an editorial decision. If a crop hides what the text refers to, or an annotation is misplaced, fix the composition. A small alignment error needs a frontend or slide correction. Do not rewrite the argument to compensate for an implementation defect.
