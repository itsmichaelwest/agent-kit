# Evidence and hierarchy

## Distinguish expression from substantiation

A scene may introduce the product, invite a feeling, explain an idea, demonstrate an action, substantiate a claim or help someone decide. These purposes can overlap. You do not need to use them all or follow them in order. A close view can make a material feel tangible. A human gesture can establish scale and familiarity. Returning quietly to a view of the whole object can give people time to absorb a detailed explanation.

Judge expressive choices by how they fit and affect the composition. Judge factual claims by the evidence that supports them. An atmospheric image does not need to prove a specification, but it cannot stand in for a test result. An evocative headline need not name a mechanism when the surrounding scene makes its meaning clear.

## Allocate attention before choosing components

Make the relative importance of the content clear on a page or in a deck. Use a few major demonstrations for the governing promise, smaller examples for supporting capabilities, and compact inventories for familiar details. An inventory can use icons and short text without giving every item a photographed scene. Do not let it replace the demonstrations that should establish the product's value.

Choose scale according to what the reader must see. A tall interface can work beside a headline; a physical transformation may need the full width. A detail crop may explain more than another whole-device image. Give the product enough prominence to draw attention where it matters and keep the relevant details legible. It does not need to occupy a fixed percentage of the canvas.

Use space to separate ideas, group related parts and give readers time to take in a subject. Compare the gaps within an argument with the gaps between arguments. A generous pause can make an encounter with the product more compelling. An empty interval with nothing worth spending time on only delays the next thought. Alternate sustained inspection with compact reading when the material calls for it.

## Develop a claim through different kinds of evidence

A sequence may show the whole product, an action or mechanism, what it changes in a task, then evidence that helps someone choose. Use the stages that help the story. A simple product may need very little explanation. A technical difference that sets the product apart may need several levels of explanation and optional detail. Returning to an earlier image or phrase can create recognition, emphasis or closure without adding a new fact.

When the product applies a rule, such as a threshold, filter or automatic choice, specific cases at the rule's boundaries often explain it better than a general statement. Show where the outcome starts, stops or changes as the input changes.

| Evidence | What it can establish | What it cannot establish by itself |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Object image or concept render | Form, arrangement, proposed finish | Tested performance, dimensions or manufacturability |
| Interface screenshot | A visible state and available controls | Response speed or successful completion of an unseen action |
| Action sequence | How an operation changes a state | Reliability across conditions absent from the demonstration |
| Matched comparison | A difference under stated conditions | Broader superiority beyond that baseline |
| Sourced measurement | The measured quantity and its conditions | A related claim about a different quantity |
| Illustration or rhetorical number | An explanation or stated proposition | An independently measured result |

For a before/after comparison, keep the subject, scale and framing stable so readers can see what changed. Name the states and let readers inspect both, whether alongside one another, on consecutive slides or through a selector. In an interactive treatment, do not let automatic progression override a manual choice.

## Connect the visual language to the product

Build the atmosphere from something meaningful: an interface surface, a material, a characteristic output, a setting where the product is used, or the feeling the campaign explores. A tint, shadow or texture can connect scenes, and a more expressive passage can briefly become the focus. Judge whether it gives this story character and draws attention to it, or could be pasted into any unrelated page.

Use actual screen states and plausible content. Crop around the relevant action, keeping enough context to explain where it happens. Consistent framing helps readers compare; varied crops help them understand. You do not need to make every typeface, radius or control novel to create an identity.

## Give the reader two useful reading speeds

Make the argument understandable to someone scanning the opening, main images and main claims. Give readers who spend more time mechanisms, consequences, examples or conditions that reward their attention. Do not simply repeat what they can scan in more words.

Choose what readers need in the main story and what they can explore in optional detail. They should be able to understand the promise without opening every disclosure. Keep conditions that matter beside the claims they affect. A simple thought can remain short when the image already explains it.

## Let interaction answer a question

Before adding a control, state the reader's question. A comparison answers what changes; a tour answers where a detail is; a tab answers how a feature serves a particular task; a disclosure answers a specific uncertainty. Keep the main argument understandable without opening every branch.

Small responses can also reward exploration, make an interaction feel tactile or make a transition feel continuous. They need not teach a new fact. Keep their prominence and duration in proportion to their role, so they do not obscure the current state or demand attention needed elsewhere.

Identify what readers must still understand without movement or interaction. Choose the stills and reading order that convey it: a transformation may need a pair of states, while a detailed object may need a single informative view. Preserve those relationships when implementing each format.

## Transfer the argument across formats

Carry the promise and evidence into each format, and choose a layout that suits it. A static deck can place matched states beside one another or on consecutive slides. An interactive deck can offer the state selector with a complete default route. You can show the range of supporting capabilities in a compact overview or appendix instead of giving every minor feature a slide. Keep qualifications with the claims in every format.
