# Identity

Your choices give the work its character. Start with the supplied brand, the user's endorsed references and the product itself. Identify what to carry forward: a reading rhythm, image treatment, sense of scale, atmosphere or way of speaking. You can build on an established direction without starting again.

## Resolve consequential choices

Use these questions to help make choices that remain open. You do not need to answer every question, and they do not limit the styles you can use.

| Choice | What to consider |
| --- | --- |
| Character | What should someone notice or feel when they first encounter the product? Which product qualities or audience situations make that intention meaningful? |
| Typography | How do the letterforms, weight, line breaks and text mass relate to the images? Does the voice benefit from familiarity, warmth, precision or tension? |
| Palette | What supports the product's colours, content and setting? Which surface changes help group content or mark a change of mood? |
| Shape and framing | Do edges, corners, borders and shadows help group the content or compete with it? Does the setting deserve to remain visible? |
| Composition | What needs to be seen together? What deserves to stand alone? Where can a stable frame make changing content easier to compare? |
| Imagery | Which viewpoint, light, scale and moment make the product compelling or understandable? Which details must remain faithful? |
| Motion | Should the response feel immediate, continuous, tactile or ceremonial here? How much of the reader's attention and time does this moment justify? |
| Voice | What would an attentive person say about this product? Where can the language be expressive, and where does the reader need literal precision? |

Record the few decisions that have a substantial effect on the result. A label such as "premium" or "minimal" does not explain what to do; a choice such as "keep the surrounding page quiet so the colourful outputs carry the character" does. Where it helps, also note what would make this direction generic and should stay out, such as a default card grid or a stock gradient. Treat those exclusions as decisions for this project, not as a list of banned ingredients. Do not mechanically derive every design token from an adjective. A useful palette need not resemble the object's physical colour, and a technical product need not use condensed type.

## Type and visual vocabulary

You can use familiar typography, quiet neutrals, rounded controls and ordinary layout patterns. Their quality depends on proportion, content and execution. A system font can suit display text as well as interface text. A distinctive typeface can add character when its shapes and rhythm suit the work, but choosing one does not automatically make the design more original.

Use fonts you have the right to use, and judge their character alongside the actual words and images. Use emphasis to establish a reading order. Keep a consistent relationship between the main thought and its supporting content; this matters more than repeating one treatment everywhere.

The product's own interface is part of its identity. When demonstrating an existing product, preserve its supplied UI, typography, colours and relevant content. Do not repaint it to match the marketing page. If the brief calls for a concept interface, design it consistently within the supplied capabilities. It may share the campaign's type and palette when that is the proposed product design. Its states and content must still behave like those of a working product. Follow [Writing](writing.md#keep-the-product-and-its-presentation-distinct) when distinguishing the intended product from the study's implementation. The surrounding frame can use the campaign's visual language without changing the product being shown.

## Composition and continuity

A centred arrangement can give an object a sense of ceremony. A split arrangement can bring a tall screen and its explanation into one view. An edge-aligned composition can give readers a clear path through the content. These are possible effects; they do not tie an arrangement to a product category. Judge the actual image and text together.

You could introduce the same compact recorder through a close view of its controls or a scene of someone recording. The close view invites inspection; the recording scene invites people to identify with the person using it. Either might use the same neutral type and quiet background. These encounters differ without requiring a change of fonts and colours.

Repeat enough visual choices for the reader to recognise them. A recurring product frame, type relationship or background treatment may give the sequence all the identity it needs. A motif can connect scenes if it belongs to the concept. It need not come from a physical feature or appear a fixed number of times. Remove it when it becomes a habit that no longer helps the work.

Use [Visual direction](visual-direction.md) to develop these choices into scenes and imagery. Choose each arrangement for what it adds to the sequence. Every chapter does not need to repeat the opening's composition.

## Resolve an open direction through visible work

When an important choice remains unresolved, show a small number of alternatives that let people see the difference. An opening and one representative scene are often enough. The alternatives might change the lead image, composition or voice while keeping much of the design system. Do not change an arbitrary number of style choices just to make the alternatives differ.

Use the brief and rendered result to recommend a direction. Ask the user to choose only when the decision still matters to the result and they have not already delegated it. A reference the user has endorsed tells you about their taste. Use that evidence without reopening the choice.

## Judge specificity

- When adapting a reference, retain useful relationships such as product scale, hierarchy, pacing and framing. Create original wording and assets, and avoid copying source marks or distinctive decorative details. You can still use general composition patterns and shared conventions.
- Imagine replacing the product. The scenes, content, demonstrations and language should need meaningful revision. The typeface or button shape may stay the same.
- Check whether repeated choices form a recognisable whole, and whether a deliberate exception improves the scene where it appears. A coherent design need not be uniform.
